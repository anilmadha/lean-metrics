How food breaks down — and what your body does with it

Digestion in one line. Your gut takes food apart into molecules small enough to pass through the intestinal wall into your blood. It starts in the mouth, keeps going in the stomach, and most of the absorbing happens in the small intestine. Each macronutrient ends up as its own building block.

Carbohydrates become glucose. Enzymes in saliva and from the pancreas cut starches and sugars down to simple sugars, mainly glucose, which the small intestine absorbs into the blood and sends to the liver. Glucose is the brain's main fuel and the fastest energy for hard training. What you don't burn right away is stored as glycogen in your muscles and liver, a limited tank; once that's full, extra carbs can be turned into fat. The U.S. Dietary Reference Intakes set 130 g a day as the minimum, based on what the brain uses.

Fiber is the carb you don't digest. Your own enzymes can't break it down, so it travels on to the large intestine, where gut bacteria ferment part of it. Along the way it slows how fast sugar reaches your blood and keeps you full longer. The target is about 14 g for every 1,000 kcal you eat. Best carb sources: oats, brown rice, potatoes, whole-grain bread, beans and lentils, fruit and vegetables. White flour and added sugar digest fastest, which is where the spike-and-crash comes from.

Protein becomes amino acids. Stomach acid and the enzyme pepsin start unraveling proteins; pancreatic enzymes finish the job in the small intestine, and the freed amino acids are absorbed into the blood. There are 20 amino acids, and 9 are essential because your body can't make them: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. They rebuild muscle and also make enzymes, hormones, antibodies and the proteins that carry oxygen and nutrients around. Your body has no dedicated protein store, which is why a steady daily supply matters. Best sources: chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt and dairy, lean meat, tofu and soy, lentils and beans. Plant eaters get every essential amino acid by eating a mix of sources across the day; they don't need to combine them at every meal.

Fats become fatty acids and glycerol. Bile from your liver and gallbladder breaks fat into tiny droplets so pancreatic lipase can split it into fatty acids and glycerol. These are absorbed through the lymph system before reaching the blood, which is a big part of why fat digests slowest and keeps you full. Fat builds every cell membrane, makes hormones such as testosterone and estrogen, and is needed to absorb vitamins A, D, E and K. At 9 kcal per gram it's also your largest energy reserve. Two fatty acids are essential: omega-3 ALA and omega-6 linoleic acid. Best sources: olive oil, avocado, nuts and seeds, eggs, and fatty fish such as salmon and sardines for the omega-3s EPA and DHA. Keep saturated fat moderate and avoid trans fats.

Vitamins, minerals and water don't need breaking down. They're absorbed as they are and work as helpers that let enzymes do their jobs. The large intestine pulls most of the remaining water back into your body and its bacteria make some vitamin K.

What happens to extra. Whatever you eat beyond what you burn gets stored, whichever macro it came from: carbs top up glycogen first and then fat, dietary fat is the easiest of the three to store as body fat, and surplus protein is burned for energy or converted rather than turned into muscle. Muscle only grows when training gives it a reason to. That's why total calories still decide weight change, while protein and training decide how much of that change is muscle versus fat.

Speed and fullness. Refined carbs empty from the stomach fastest, protein slower, fat slowest. A plate that pairs protein, fiber and some fat flattens the blood-sugar curve and keeps you full for hours, which makes a calorie deficit much easier to hold. The NIDDK guide to the digestive system walks through each organ's role.

Why protein matters

Muscle repair and growth. Protein breaks down into amino acids — the building blocks your body uses to repair exercise-damaged muscle fibers and build new tissue. This is the basis of getting stronger from training, not just lifting itself.

Not just muscle. Protein also forms enzymes, hormones, antibodies, skin, hair, and the structural proteins in bone and connective tissue. It's functional material the body runs on, not only a muscle-building nutrient.

Preserving lean mass in a deficit. When you eat fewer calories than you burn, your body pulls energy from both fat and muscle. Adequate protein (see your target on the Setup tab) signals the body to protect muscle and lose fat preferentially, per the ISSN position stand this app's targets are based on.

Satiety. Gram for gram, protein is the most filling macronutrient — part of why higher-protein diets tend to make a calorie deficit easier to stick to.

Why spread protein across meals

Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) works meal by meal, not as a daily average. Your body raises its rate of building new muscle protein for a few hours after a protein-containing meal, then it tapers back down. Hitting your day's total in one or two huge meals leaves long stretches with little stimulus.

Roughly 3–4 even meals beat 1–2 big ones. Studies comparing protein distribution patterns (e.g. Mamerow et al. and similar work) found that spreading protein evenly across ~4 meals stimulated more muscle protein synthesis over a day than the same total skewed toward one large meal.

Practical target. Aim for roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg bodyweight (about 20–40g for most adults) of protein per meal, 3–4 times a day, rather than saving it all for dinner.

Leucine & mTOR — the "switch" for muscle building

mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) is a cellular pathway that acts like a master switch for muscle protein synthesis — when it's activated, your cells ramp up building new protein.

Leucine is the trigger. Of the essential amino acids, leucine is uniquely effective at switching on the mTORC1 pathway. Most research on the "leucine threshold" points to roughly 2–3g of leucine in a meal as the amount needed to maximally trigger this response.

Where to get it. Animal proteins (whey, eggs, chicken, beef, fish, dairy) and soy are leucine-dense — about 25–30g of these proteins usually clears the threshold. Many plant proteins (beans, grains, nuts) are lower in leucine per gram, so slightly larger portions help plant-based eaters hit the same trigger.

Why this connects to meal timing. This is the mechanism behind spreading protein across meals above — each meal needs enough leucine to switch mTOR back on, which is why 3–4 threshold-clearing meals outperform one giant one for muscle building.

How alcohol affects your body

Pure calories, no nutrition. Alcohol provides about 7 kcal/gram — nearly as dense as fat (9 kcal/g) — with no protein, fiber, or, in most drinks, meaningful vitamins or minerals.

Your liver stops everything else to deal with it. Alcohol is treated as a metabolic priority: the liver shifts to clearing it almost immediately, which pauses fat oxidation while that's happening — a big part of why drinking can stall fat-loss progress even on an otherwise on-plan day.

It blunts muscle protein synthesis. Research shows alcohol can suppress the muscle-building response to protein and exercise by a meaningful margin after heavier intake, independent of the extra calories involved.

Sleep and recovery take a hit. Alcohol can help you fall asleep faster but fragments sleep later in the night and suppresses REM sleep — the stage most tied to recovery and memory — so both training and weight-loss progress suffer the next day.

Dehydration. Alcohol suppresses the hormone that helps your kidneys retain water, increasing fluid loss — part of why a drink often leaves you thirstier the next morning.

Current guidance. NIAAA defines a "standard drink" as about 14g of pure alcohol — roughly a 12oz beer, 5oz wine, or 1.5oz spirits. Older U.S. guidance set moderate drinking at up to 1 drink/day for women and 2/day for men; the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans moved away from a specific numeric ceiling and now simply advise drinking less, reflecting growing evidence that no amount is clearly risk-free.

THC & cannabis — what it does to your body

How it works. THC (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol) is the main psychoactive compound in cannabis. It acts on CB1 receptors in the body's endocannabinoid system — the same network that helps regulate appetite, memory, mood, pain and sleep — which is why its effects show up in so many places at once. Per the CDC, it directly affects the parts of the brain that handle memory, attention, decision-making, coordination and reaction time.

Appetite is the biggest fat-loss risk. THC switches on hunger signaling in the brain ("the munchies"), and the cravings usually lean toward calorie-dense snacks. One evening can quietly add several hundred unplanned calories, so log it like any other food and your Today numbers stay honest.

Training and recovery. It won't make you fitter or stronger. A 2020 systematic review found no performance benefit from regular use, and no clear performance harm either in the few small studies available. In the moment, though, THC raises heart rate and slows reaction time and coordination, which is a poor match for heavy lifts or technical work. Many users say they recover better; that hasn't shown up in measured outcomes.

Sleep. It can make falling asleep feel easier, but a 2025 meta-analysis of sleep-lab studies found no consistent improvement in sleep structure. Tolerance builds with regular use. The most consistent finding came after stopping: shorter sleep, taking longer to fall asleep, and vivid dreams (REM rebound) for days to weeks.

Heart and lungs. THC raises heart rate and blood pressure shortly after use. In an American Heart Association study of 434,104 U.S. adults, daily users had 25% higher odds of heart attack and 42% higher odds of stroke than non-users, and the link held in people who had never used tobacco. Smoked cannabis harms lung tissue however it's smoked. Edibles spare the lungs but can take up to 2 hours to kick in, which is why people re-dose too early and overdo it.

Mood and dependence. The CDC estimates about 3 in 10 people who use cannabis have cannabis use disorder — trouble cutting back despite problems it's causing. Risk climbs with daily use, starting young, and higher potency: average THC roughly doubled from 9% in 2008 to 17% in 2017, and many dispensary products now run above 20%. Heavy use is also linked to anxiety, depression and, with frequent high-potency use, psychosis.

Two warning signs. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome is repeated bouts of severe nausea and vomiting in long-term heavy users, often briefly eased by hot showers; it only resolves by stopping. And don't drive high: THC slows reaction time and decision-making, and adding alcohol makes it worse.

Cutting back or quitting. Withdrawal is real but short-lived. Irritability, restlessness, low appetite, cravings and poor sleep usually start within 1–3 days, peak in the first week, and ease over roughly 1–2 weeks (sleep can take a little longer). Regular exercise, a fixed sleep schedule and the Meditate tab's wind-down timer all help through that stretch. For free, confidential help finding support, call SAMHSA's National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357.

Essential vitamins

Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K). Stored in body fat and the liver, absorbed best alongside dietary fat. Vitamin D (bone health, immune function) and vitamin K (blood clotting, bone) are the two most commonly under-target with low sun exposure or very low-fat diets.

Water-soluble (B-complex, C). Not stored in meaningful amounts, so they need regular intake. B12 (nerve function, red blood cells) is the one most worth watching if you eat little or no animal product, since it's almost exclusively found in animal foods.

Minerals — magnesium, zinc, iron, calcium, potassium, sodium and the trace minerals — have their own card just below, with daily targets and food sources.

Full detail on any of these — daily targets, deficiency signs, upper limits — is in the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements' consumer fact sheets.

Minerals: magnesium, zinc & the rest

Why they matter. Minerals don't give you energy themselves, but almost every step that turns food into energy, contracts a muscle, or builds new tissue needs one. Training and dieting both raise the stakes: you lose some in sweat, and eating less food means less room to get them in. Targets below are U.S. daily values from the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS).

Magnesium

What it does. Helps run more than 300 enzyme systems, including the ones that make ATP (your cells' energy currency), and is needed for muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signals, blood-sugar control and bone. ODS notes that many people in the U.S. get less than recommended.

How much. Men 400 mg a day (420 mg from age 31); women 310 mg (320 mg from 31).

Best sources. Pumpkin seeds (about 156 mg per ounce), chia seeds (111 mg/oz), almonds (80 mg/oz), cooked spinach (78 mg per half cup), cashews, black beans, edamame, whole grains and dark chocolate. A handful of seeds or nuts a day closes most of the gap.

Supplements. The upper limit is 350 mg a day from supplements (food doesn't count toward it); going over mainly causes diarrhea and cramping. Citrate and glycinate absorb better than oxide. For sleep, the evidence is thin: NIH's NCCIH says a 2021 review of three small, low-quality trials in older adults found people may fall asleep a little faster, which isn't enough to rely on.

Zinc

What it does. Immune function, wound healing, building protein and DNA, taste and smell, and making testosterone and sperm. A real shortfall clearly lowers testosterone (see the testosterone card), which is why zinc turns up in almost every "booster".

How much. Men 11 mg a day; women 8 mg. Vegetarians may need up to 50% more, because compounds in grains and beans (phytates) block some absorption.

Best sources. Oysters are in a league of their own: one serving covers several days. Then red meat (about 3–5 mg per 3 oz), crab and lobster, poultry, pork and fortified cereals, with smaller amounts in beans, nuts, pumpkin seeds, whole grains, eggs and dairy.

Don't overdo it. The upper limit is 40 mg a day from all sources. ODS warns that long-term excess lowers copper, weakens immunity and lowers HDL ("good") cholesterol. Typical ZMA blends supply about 30 mg zinc and 450 mg magnesium, so with food you can pass the zinc limit, and the magnesium is above the 350 mg supplement limit on its own.

Iron

Carries oxygen in your blood; low iron shows up as fatigue, breathlessness and workouts that feel harder than they should. Men need 8 mg a day; women 18 mg until menopause, then 8 mg. Red meat, poultry and fish give the best-absorbed (heme) form; beans, lentils, tofu, spinach and fortified cereals absorb better with vitamin C and worse with tea or coffee at the same meal. Vegetarians need about 1.8 times as much. Get a blood test before supplementing: too much iron is harmful, and the upper limit is 45 mg a day (ODS).

Calcium

Bone strength, plus every muscle contraction and heartbeat. 1,000 mg a day for most adults; 1,200 mg for women from 51 and men from 71. Dairy, fortified plant milks, calcium-set tofu, canned sardines or salmon with the bones, kale and bok choy. Large doses taken with an iron-rich meal reduce iron absorption, so split them up (ODS).

Potassium & sodium

Potassium balances fluids and counters sodium's effect on blood pressure. Targets are 3,400 mg a day for men and 2,600 mg for women, and most people fall short. Potatoes, beans, lentils, bananas, dried apricots, yogurt, squash and leafy greens are the easy wins (ODS). If you have kidney disease or take blood-pressure medication, ask before using potassium supplements or salt substitutes.

Sodium is the one most people get too much of: keep it under 2,300 mg a day (the ceiling on the Setup tab). Heavy sweaters are the exception. In a study of 506 athletes, sweat averaged about 1.2 litres an hour and roughly 830 mg of sodium per litre (anywhere from about 420 to 1,630 mg). For workouts under an hour, water and normal meals cover it. For long or hot sessions with a lot of sweat, a salty meal afterwards or an electrolyte drink helps.

The trace minerals

Iodine (150 mcg a day) is needed to make thyroid hormone, which sets your metabolic rate. Iodized salt, dairy, eggs and seafood supply it; if you only cook with sea salt or kosher salt, you may be getting very little (ODS).

Selenium (55 mcg a day) supports thyroid function and antioxidant defenses. Seafood, eggs, meat and whole grains are steady sources. Brazil nuts are so rich (about 544 mcg per ounce, or 6–8 nuts) that one or two a day is plenty; the upper limit is 400 mcg (ODS).

Vitamin D isn't a mineral, but you can't absorb calcium well without it: 600 IU a day (800 IU over 70), with an upper limit of 4,000 IU. Fatty fish, fortified milk and sunlight are the main sources (ODS).

Food first, test before you supplement. A varied diet with meat or fish, dairy (or fortified alternatives), beans, nuts, seeds, whole grains and plenty of vegetables covers nearly all of these. Supplements make sense for a measured shortfall or a diet that leaves an obvious gap, such as B12 or zinc on a vegan diet or vitamin D in winter. Supplements worth considering

Protein powder (whey or plant-based). Not essential if food already hits your gram target, but the easiest way to close a gap — after training or when a meal falls short. Whey digests fast (good post-workout or first thing in the morning); casein digests slowly (useful before bed for an overnight supply).

Creatine monohydrate. One of the most well-researched supplements in sports nutrition — about 3–5g/day, every day, improves strength and power output. Timing barely matters since it works by saturating muscle stores over time; take it with any meal, consistently, rather than specifically around training.

Vitamin D3. Worth considering with limited sun exposure — supports bone health and immune function. Take it with a meal containing some fat, since it's fat-soluble and absorbs better that way. Typical over-the-counter doses run 1,000–2,000 IU/day; get your level checked before going higher.

Omega-3 / fish oil. Supports heart and brain health if you don't eat fatty fish a couple times a week. Take it with meals — it reduces the "fishy burps" some people get on an empty stomach.

Magnesium. Worth it if your diet is light on nuts, seeds, beans and greens, since many people fall short. Glycinate and citrate absorb well; keep supplements to 350 mg a day or less. Evening is fine, though the evidence that it improves sleep is weak. Targets and food sources are in the minerals card above.

Caffeine. Reliably improves focus and workout performance taken 30–60 minutes beforehand. Dose, timing and daily limits are covered in the caffeine card below.

Supplements fill gaps in a diet — they don't fix a bad one. None of the above are essential if food already covers it, and as always, check with a doctor before starting anything new, especially if you take medication or have a health condition.

Caffeine: dose, timing & limits

How it works. Caffeine blocks adenosine, the chemical that builds up through the day and makes you feel sleepy, so you feel alert and hard efforts feel easier. It masks tiredness rather than removing it, which is why the crash arrives when it wears off.

For training. The ISSN position stand on caffeine puts the reliably effective range at about 3–6 mg per kg of bodyweight, taken 30–60 minutes before exercise, and doses as low as about 2 mg/kg can still help. For a 75 kg adult that's roughly 150–225 mg at the low end, about one or two cups of coffee. More isn't better: higher doses mostly add jitters and a worse night's sleep.

Daily ceiling. The FDA cites about 400 mg a day, roughly two to three 12-oz cups of coffee, as generally not linked to negative effects in healthy adults. Sensitivity varies a lot; pregnancy, heart-rhythm problems, anxiety and some medications all call for less.

Timing protects your sleep. Caffeine's half-life is about 3–5 hours, per the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and in one study a dose 6 hours before bed still cut total sleep by about an hour. A good rule of thumb is to keep it to the morning and early afternoon, around 8 hours before you plan to sleep. An evening workout is the place to skip the pre-workout.

Know where it's hiding. Energy drinks range from about 54 to 328 mg per 16 oz, and many pre-workouts carry 200 mg or more a scoop, so it's easy to stack past 400 mg without noticing. Avoid pure or powdered caffeine entirely: the FDA notes that around 1,200 mg, less than half a teaspoon, can cause seizures.

Hydration and tolerance. Moderate coffee and tea still count toward your daily fluids, since the mild diuretic effect is largely offset by the water in the drink. Daily use builds tolerance; if it's stopped doing much, stepping down over a week or two restores the effect (expect a few days of headaches).

Sleep: the most underrated recovery tool

How much you actually need. CDC and sleep-medicine guidance puts healthy adults at 7–9 hours a night. Falling short regularly is linked to worse recovery, worse appetite control, and worse training performance — "I function fine on 5" is usually adaptation to feeling bad, not evidence it's harmless.

Deep sleep repairs the body. Growth hormone release — the hormone most responsible for tissue repair and muscle recovery — peaks during deep (slow-wave) sleep, concentrated in the first half of the night. Cutting sleep short disproportionately cuts into this stage.

REM sleep repairs the mind. Later sleep cycles are REM-heavy — tied to memory consolidation, learning, and emotional regulation. Alcohol and short sleep duration both suppress REM disproportionately (see the alcohol card above).

It directly affects fat loss and hunger. Sleep-restriction studies found that cutting sleep to ~5.5 hours while dieting shifted weight loss away from fat and toward lean mass, and raised hunger hormones — the same calorie deficit performed noticeably worse on too little sleep.

Practical sleep hygiene. A consistent sleep/wake time (even on weekends), a cool dark room, cutting caffeine by early afternoon, and keeping screens and bright light out of the last 30–60 minutes before bed are the highest-leverage, best-evidenced habits — more impactful than any supplement. The Meditate tab's timer can help wind down before bed.

Testosterone & your other hormones

What testosterone does. In men it drives muscle and bone strength, red blood cell production, sex drive and sperm production, and it affects mood and energy. Women make it too, in much smaller amounts (most of it is converted to estradiol), and need it for sex drive, energy, and bone and muscle strength (Cleveland Clinic).

What's normal. A large 2017 Endocrine Society study of more than 9,000 men set the normal range for healthy, non-obese men aged 19–39 at 264–916 ng/dL. The American Urological Association treats a total testosterone below about 300 ng/dL as low, but only diagnoses deficiency when it's low on two early-morning tests and there are symptoms. Symptoms such as low sex drive, erection problems, fatigue, low mood, losing muscle or gaining fat are vague and have many other causes. Levels are highest in the morning and fall by about 1–2% a year with age.

What lowers it, and what you can do

Short sleep. In a University of Chicago study (JAMA, 2011), healthy young men who slept under 5 hours a night for one week had 10–15% less testosterone, about what 10–15 years of aging would do. This is the cheapest fix there is: 7–9 hours.

Extra body fat. Fat tissue converts testosterone into estrogen, and obesity suppresses the signal that tells the testes to make more. In a meta-analysis of 24 studies, losing weight raised testosterone, and the more weight people lost, the bigger the rise.

Eating too little for too long. Under-eating relative to your training (what the IOC calls low energy availability) suppresses reproductive hormones in both men and women, along with bone health and performance. That's one reason this app keeps a calorie floor and warns when you're losing more than about 1% of bodyweight a week.

Very low-fat diets. A 2021 meta-analysis (Whittaker & Wu) of six studies found men on low-fat diets (about 19% of calories from fat) had lower testosterone than on higher-fat diets (about 39%). Your targets keep fat at 22–30% of calories with a minimum per kg of bodyweight.

Heavy drinking. Chronic heavy alcohol use lowers testosterone and can shrink the testes and reduce fertility (NIAAA review). A few drinks a week isn't the issue; regular heavy nights are.

A real zinc shortfall. In a small study, 20 weeks of a low-zinc diet dropped young men's testosterone from about 40 to 11 nmol/L, and zinc supplements roughly doubled it in older men who were mildly deficient. Extra zinc doesn't raise testosterone if you already get enough.

Lifting helps, but not through post-workout spikes. Strength training builds muscle and helps you lose fat, both of which support healthy levels. The brief hormone rise after a workout doesn't decide how much muscle you build, though: McMaster University studies found no link between post-exercise testosterone and muscle or strength gains, and men and women built new muscle protein at the same rate after training despite a 45-fold difference in the testosterone rise.

Boosters, TRT and steroids

"Testosterone boosters" mostly don't deliver. A USC review of 50 top-selling products (World Journal of Men's Health) found about 90% claimed to boost testosterone, but fewer than 25% had any research behind their claims, and some contained vitamins and minerals above safe upper limits.

Testosterone therapy (TRT) is a prescription treatment for diagnosed deficiency. It switches off your own production and lowers sperm count, so it isn't a performance shortcut, and anyone hoping to have children should discuss that first.

Steroids and SARMs. Products sold for bodybuilding that contain steroids or SARMs carry serious risks: the FDA warns of heart attack, stroke, blood clots, liver and kidney injury, psychosis, infertility and testicular shrinkage.

Other hormones that shape your results

Cortisol is the stress hormone. A rise during and after a hard workout is normal; what hurts is staying high from chronic stress and short sleep, which pushes up appetite and makes recovery harder.

Insulin moves glucose and amino acids into muscle after a meal. Strength training, regular walking (your 10,000 steps) and losing excess fat all make your body more sensitive to it.

Thyroid hormones set your metabolic rate, and they need iodine and selenium (see the minerals card). Unexplained fatigue, feeling cold, or weight changes that don't match your logging are worth a blood test (TSH) with your doctor.

Leptin and ghrelin run hunger. Leptin falls and ghrelin rises when you diet or sleep too little, which is why hunger climbs late in a cut. Enough protein, fiber and sleep, plus a moderate rather than extreme deficit, keep them in check.

Estrogen and progesterone shift across the menstrual cycle and can move scale weight by a pound or two of water within a month. Compare weigh-ins from the same phase of each cycle, and trust the 7-day average in Trends over any single day.

If you think your hormones are off, see a doctor for a proper blood test before trying to fix it with supplements. This card is general education, not a diagnosis.

How smoking affects your body — and how to quit

It directly undercuts training and recovery. Carbon monoxide from smoke binds red blood cells more readily than oxygen does, measurably reducing how much oxygen your blood delivers to working muscle — this shows up as lower endurance and a harder time recovering between sets or sessions.

Cardiovascular cost. Smoking raises heart rate and blood pressure, damages the lining of blood vessels, and accelerates plaque buildup — the same process behind heart attack and stroke risk, compounding with the extra cardiovascular strain of carrying excess weight.

It slows healing and muscle repair. Reduced oxygen delivery and chronic low-grade inflammation from smoking measurably slow wound and tissue healing — relevant for anyone training hard enough to generate normal muscle micro-damage that needs to repair.

Vaping isn't a free pass. Nicotine itself — combusted or vaped — raises heart rate and blood pressure and is addictive. Vaping removes much of the tar and combustion-byproduct exposure, but the cardiovascular and addiction effects of nicotine remain.

How to quit — what actually works. The combination with the best track record is nicotine replacement (patch, gum, or lozenge) or a prescription medication (varenicline or bupropion, via a doctor) plus behavioral support — a quitline, app, or counselor roughly doubles success rates over willpower alone. Smokefree.gov and the U.S. 1-800-QUIT-NOW line are free starting points.

It gets better fast. Heart rate and blood pressure drop within 20 minutes of the last cigarette; circulation and lung function measurably improve within weeks to months; within a year, excess heart disease risk is roughly cut in half. It's never too late to see gains from quitting.

Blood tests worth doing: the ten markers that matter

Why these ten. A standard "annual physical" panel usually checks total cholesterol, glucose and a blood count — and misses most of what actually predicts heart disease, insulin resistance, low energy and poor recovery. The ten markers below are the ones that change how you eat, train and supplement. All of them are ordinary lab tests (nothing exotic), they are cheap when bundled, and most are best done fasted in the morning (water only, 10–12 hours). The ranges here are general reference points from the bodies linked under each marker; your lab's own range and your doctor's read of the whole picture come first. One result is a snapshot — a trend over two or three tests is what tells you something.

ApoB (apolipoprotein B)

What it is. Every particle that can drive plaque into an artery wall — LDL, VLDL, IDL and Lp(a) — carries exactly one ApoB protein, so ApoB is a direct count of the plaque-building particles in your blood. It beats LDL-cholesterol as a risk marker because two people with the same LDL-C can have very different particle counts (ESC/EAS 2019 lipid guideline).

Ranges. Lab "normal" is often up to 130 mg/dL. For long-term heart health most lipid specialists want under 90 mg/dL, under 80 if you already have risk factors (high blood pressure, family history, diabetes), and under 65 after a cardiac event.

What moves it. Replacing saturated fat (ghee, butter, fatty meat, coconut oil) with olive oil, nuts and fish; 25–30 g of fibre a day (oats, dal, beans — soluble fibre binds cholesterol); losing visceral fat; and, when lifestyle is not enough, statins or ezetimibe, which lower ApoB reliably. Retest 8–12 weeks after a change, then yearly.

Lipoprotein(a) — "Lp little a"

What it is. An LDL particle with an extra protein (apo(a)) stuck to it that makes it both plaque-building and clot-promoting. The level is about 90% inherited and barely changes with diet, exercise or statins, which is why it is tested once in a lifetime and why roughly one in five people are walking around with a high value they have never heard of (EAS 2022 consensus).

Ranges. Under 30 mg/dL (under 75 nmol/L) is low risk; 30–50 mg/dL is borderline; over 50 mg/dL (over 125 nmol/L) is high and over 90 mg/dL very high. Ask for the nmol/L assay if you can — it counts particles rather than weight.

What moves it. Almost nothing you can do at home, so a high result means being stricter with everything else that is in your control: ApoB, blood pressure, blood sugar, smoking. Lipoprotein apheresis and new RNA-based drugs (in late trials) are the medical options; niacin lowers it but has not shown a benefit. If yours is high, first-degree relatives should be tested.

Triglycerides

What they are. The main form of fat in blood — one glycerol with three fatty acids — carrying energy from the liver and gut to muscle and fat cells. A fasting level is really a readout of how your liver is handling carbohydrate, alcohol and total calories (AHA).

Ranges. Under 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) is "normal"; under 100 mg/dL is the optimal zone; 150–199 borderline; 200–499 high; 500+ risks pancreatitis. A triglyceride-to-HDL ratio above 3 (mg/dL) is an early sign of insulin resistance.

What moves it. This is the most diet-responsive number on the list: cutting sugar, sweet drinks, juice, refined flour and white rice, limiting alcohol (even moderate drinking raises it), losing 5% of bodyweight, daily walking, and oily fish or 2–4 g of EPA+DHA for high values. Changes show within weeks; retest after 6–8 weeks.

Fasting insulin

What it is. How much insulin your pancreas needs to pump to keep fasting glucose normal. Glucose and HbA1c can stay in range for a decade while insulin quietly climbs — this test catches insulin resistance 10–15 years before "pre-diabetes" shows up (ADA Standards of Care discuss insulin resistance; HOMA-IR is the usual index).

Ranges. Labs quote up to about 25 µIU/mL as normal, which is far too generous. Metabolically healthy people usually sit between 2 and 8 µIU/mL; 8–12 is early resistance; above 12–15 is clear resistance. HOMA-IR (fasting insulin × fasting glucose in mg/dL ÷ 405) under 1.0 is ideal, over 2.0 is resistant.

What moves it. Muscle is the biggest glucose sink, so strength training 2–3 times a week and 8,000+ steps are the strongest levers; then fewer refined carbs and sugary drinks, protein and fibre at each meal, 7+ hours of sleep (one short night measurably raises insulin), and visceral-fat loss. Walks after meals help immediately. Retest every 3–6 months while improving, then yearly.

hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein)

What it is. A liver protein that rises with inflammation. The "high-sensitivity" assay reads the low, chronic, silent levels linked to artery disease rather than the big spikes of an infection (AHA/CDC statement).

Ranges. Under 1.0 mg/L is low cardiovascular risk; 1.0–3.0 average; over 3.0 high. Over 10 means an acute infection or injury — retest in 2–3 weeks once you are well; a cold, a hard training week or a tattoo will all push it up temporarily.

What moves it. Visceral fat is the main driver, so waist loss is first; then sleep, stopping smoking, limiting alcohol, oily fish or omega-3, a diet high in vegetables, legumes and olive oil, and treating gum disease or sleep apnoea. Most people with a high value also have high triglycerides and fasting insulin — fix those and CRP follows.

Homocysteine

What it is. An amino acid produced while your body recycles methionine. Clearing it needs folate, B12 and B6; when any of those is short — or you carry a common MTHFR variant — homocysteine builds up and irritates blood-vessel linings. Raised levels track with heart disease, stroke, dementia and poor bone health (NIH ODS).

Ranges. Labs flag over 15 µmol/L. Under 10 µmol/L is the usual optimal target and many clinicians prefer under 8; 10–15 is mildly raised.

What moves it. Mostly B vitamins: folate from dal, chickpeas, spinach and edamame (or methylfolate), B12 (see below — vegetarians are the classic case), and B6 from chicken, fish, potatoes and bananas. Coffee, alcohol and smoking raise it; creatine supplementation lowers it slightly. Retest 8–12 weeks after starting B vitamins.

Omega-3 index

What it is. The share of EPA and DHA in your red-blood-cell membranes — a three-month average of how much long-chain omega-3 you actually absorb, far more useful than guessing from fish-oil capsules (NIH ODS). A plain "omega-3 blood test" without the index is less useful.

Ranges. 8% or higher is associated with the lowest cardiac risk; 4–8% is intermediate; under 4% is high risk. Most people eating little fish test at 3–5%.

What moves it. Two servings of oily fish a week (salmon, sardines, mackerel, rohu to a lesser extent) or 1–2 g of combined EPA+DHA daily from fish or algae oil; it takes about three months to shift. Plant omega-3 (ALA from flax, chia, walnuts) converts poorly — under 10% — so it does not raise the index much on its own.

Vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D)

What it is. The storage form of a hormone made in skin from sunlight and absorbed from oily fish, eggs and fortified milk; it governs calcium absorption, bone, muscle function, immunity and mood. Indoor work, darker skin, sunscreen, winter and living far from the equator all push it down, so deficiency is common even in sunny countries (NIH ODS).

Ranges. Under 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) is deficient; 20–30 insufficient; 30–50 ng/mL (75–125 nmol/L) is the range most clinicians aim for; above 100 ng/mL risks toxicity.

What moves it. 15–30 minutes of midday sun on arms and legs most days, oily fish, and D3 at 1,000–2,000 IU a day with a fatty meal (4,000 IU is the daily upper limit without supervision). Every 1,000 IU a day raises the level by roughly 8–10 ng/mL over 2–3 months; retest after 3 months, then once a year at the end of winter.

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

What it is. Needed for red blood cells, DNA synthesis, nerve insulation and clearing homocysteine. It comes only from animal foods and fortified products, and absorption falls with age, metformin, acid-reducing drugs and gut problems, so vegetarians and anyone over 50 should check it (NIH ODS).

Ranges. Labs call 200–900 pg/mL normal, but symptoms (fatigue, tingling, poor memory, low mood) appear well inside that band. Above 400–500 pg/mL is the comfortable zone; 200–300 is a grey area worth confirming with methylmalonic acid (MMA) or homocysteine, which rise when B12 is functionally short.

What moves it. Eggs, milk, curd, paneer, fish and meat; on a vegetarian or vegan diet, 25–100 mcg a day or 1,000 mcg two or three times a week of cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin. Absorption from a tablet is only a few percent, which is why the doses look large. Retest after 3 months; injections are for confirmed malabsorption.

Serum TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)

What it is. The pituitary's signal to the thyroid. When thyroid hormone (T3/T4) is low the pituitary shouts louder, so a high TSH means an underactive thyroid — slow metabolism, weight gain that does not respond to dieting, cold hands, fatigue, hair loss, raised cholesterol. A very low TSH means overactive (American Thyroid Association).

Ranges. Lab range is roughly 0.4–4.0 mIU/L (some use 0.4–4.5). Many endocrinologists consider 0.5–2.5 mIU/L the optimal zone for someone with symptoms; 2.5–4.0 with symptoms or thyroid antibodies is worth watching; over 4.0 twice is subclinical hypothyroidism. Test in the morning, before any thyroid medication, and not during or right after an illness or a crash diet (both move it).

What moves it. Adequate iodine (iodised salt, dairy, eggs, seafood), selenium (one or two Brazil nuts a day), not starving yourself (very low-calorie diets lower T3 and raise TSH), sleep and iron status. Biotin supplements can falsely distort thyroid tests — stop them for two days before the draw. If TSH is high, add free T4 and thyroid antibodies (TPO) to the next test.

Other things worth knowing

Fiber feeds more than digestion. Beyond regularity, fiber slows glucose absorption (steadier energy, less of a blood-sugar spike) and feeds the gut bacteria linked to immune and metabolic health — the reasoning behind your fiber target on the Setup tab.

Food first, supplements second. Supplements fill gaps; they don't replace a varied diet of whole foods, which brings fiber, phytonutrients, and a wider spread of vitamins and minerals that pills alone don't fully replicate.

These are population-level summaries of established nutrition research, not a medical assessment. If you have a health condition, are pregnant, or are under 18, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing your diet, supplements, alcohol or cannabis use, or smoking cessation plan.

How this is calculated

BMR uses the Mifflin–St Jeor equation, generally regarded as the most accurate resting-metabolism estimate for most adults: 10×weight(kg) + 6.25×height(cm) − 5×age + 5 (men) or −161 (women). If you enter a body-fat %, it switches to Katch–McArdle (370 + 21.6×lean mass in kg), which accounts for how much of your weight is muscle.

Use my steps (Activity level) builds maintenance from your own numbers instead of a broad level: BMR × 1.15 (digestion and everyday movement other than walking), plus your average steps × 0.0005 kcal per kg of bodyweight per step (about 37 kcal per 1,000 steps at 75 kg), plus workouts: sessions per week × 1.4 / 3.5 / 7 kcal per kg for light / moderate / hard, spread over 7 days. Those per-kg figures come from standard exercise MET values, counting only the energy above resting. With about 3,000 steps and no workouts it lands right on the standard sedentary factor (1.2); for more active people it usually comes out a little lower than the broad levels, which tend to overestimate.

TDEE multiplies BMR by an activity factor (1.2–1.9) that covers all your daily movement — steps and workouts together. Because the target already includes your training, don't add exercise calories from a watch back on top. If you lose faster or slower than predicted for 2–3 weeks, adjust the activity level; real metabolism varies by 5–15%.

Calorie target adjusts TDEE up or down based on your goal, with a safety floor for fat-loss goals of 1,200 kcal (women) or 1,500 kcal (men), never above 90% of maintenance. Goals step down evenly: 10%, 20% and 30% below maintenance for mild, standard and aggressive fat loss, and about 10% above it for lean gain. It estimates weekly weight change using the standard ≈7,700 kcal-per-kg energy equivalence.

Protein follows the International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand — 1.4–2.0 g/kg for general training, and up to 2.3–3.1 g/kg of lean mass in a deficit to protect muscle. If you enter body fat %, protein is set on lean mass instead (the higher 2.3–3.1 g/kg range for fat-loss goals). Without it, anyone above a BMI of 25 gets protein calculated on a reference weight (the weight at BMI 25, or goal weight if higher), since per-kg targets on full bodyweight overshoot.

Fat targets 22–30% of calories, with a floor of about 0.6 g/kg bodyweight to protect hormone health, per the ISSN position stand on diets and body composition and USDA dietary fat guidance (20–35% AMDR).

Carbs fill the remaining calories. A note appears if that falls under ~130 g/day, the amount the brain needs from glucose per Institute of Medicine guidance.

Measured maintenance (Trends tab) looks at the last 28 days: your average logged intake, minus the energy implied by your weight trend (about 7,700 kcal per kg). With at least 14 full days of food logs and 8 weigh-ins spread over two weeks it's usually more accurate than any formula, as long as you log everything. You can switch your targets to it, and back, at any time.

Waist-to-height ratio uses your latest waist from the Today check-in. NICE treats 0.5–0.59 as increased health risk and 0.6+ as higher risk; it's a better signal than BMI for people carrying muscle.

Water uses a common rule of thumb of about 33 ml per kg of bodyweight, plus an activity bump (0.1–1.1 L) for your selected activity level. Hotter climates, illness, or breastfeeding raise this further.

Fiber uses the widely cited 14 g per 1,000 kcal rule of thumb, but never less than the Institute of Medicine Adequate Intake for your sex and age (38 g men / 25 g women, or 30 g / 21 g over 50), so a lower calorie target doesn't shrink it.

Sodium and added sugar are shown as ceilings, not targets: 2,300 mg/day sodium (FDA) and 25 g/day for women or 36 g/day for men on added sugar (American Heart Association) — lower if you have high blood pressure or your doctor advises otherwise. Added sugar you log with the spoon add-ons (sugar, jaggery, honey, ketchup) is totalled on the Today tab against this ceiling; sugar inside other foods and sodium aren't tracked, so treat those as things to keep an eye on.

Food-log macros come from a small built-in reference list of common foods (typical USDA-style values) or whatever you enter manually — they're estimates, not lab measurements.

These are population-level formulas, not a medical assessment. Individual metabolism varies by 5–15% or more. If you have a health condition, are pregnant, or are under 18, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing your diet.
